I try to keep current events out of the 'something I hated' section of my weekly blog posts because there's just too much awful shit happening. Otherwise, I'd put a new horrible A.I. story in there every week. But now it's getting really pervasive and personal.

## prelude: they fired the only Black person in my unit and replaced them with A.I.

I like my boss. He's not the one who fired my Black nonbinary coworker. But he is the one who decided that we would throw all the work they did and replace it with A.I. slop.

The coworker recorded interviews with social workers in a southern state. Frankly, the footage wasn't great-- it was poorly lit and the subject weren't mic'd up correctly. But it was real. My boss decided we were going to use A.I. to make it 'better' somehow. This turned out to be by taking everything the subjects said and putting it in the mouths of A.I.-generated avatars. These avatars would look more "professional" compared to the shoddy interviews and Zoom screen captures of the real social workers.

The social workers also all happened to be Black women. My boss said, "this'll be the one case where adding a white man makes the video _more_ diverse". So we did. I transcribed the interview, fed it into an A.I. service, and gave it a white Chad's face. "It's so meaningful to me to get to work with these clients and hear their stories," Chad said.

This was the first time at my job where I felt in crisis. I work for a subdivision of a university that focuses on professional development. We work a lot with my state's Department of Human Services. It is a 'woke' workplace. The structures of the state and the university system aside, I at least feel like my work is worthwhile. This was not worthwhile. Replacing the real voices and faces of social workers with 'professional'-looking slop avatars made me sick. It still does. 

I handed the final products back to my boss, and he forwarded them to the client. I haven't heard what they said, and I've never asked.

## computer, give the victim down syndrome

I also support a unit that develops trainings for people that investigate abuse against disabled people. These people have to be trained to be aware of their physical position in every room they enter. They have carry sterile rulers to include for scale in the photos they take of open wounds. They have to learn how get information from nonverbal victims that have been raped by their primary caregivers.

Recently, I've been working with the unit that makes these trainings on a routine refresh of some learning modules. Originally, back in 2024, I used text-to-speech to narrate the  presentations. I feel bad about that. I justified it to myself by saying that TTS has been around a lot longer than LLMs and Stable Diffusion models, and it's not generating anything new that wasn't in the script. But now that I was invited to re-do the content, I decided I was going to strip out the TTS and replace it with real narration, courtesy of my coworkers who are happy to volunteer their time and voices. When I told the project lead, she scheduled a meeting with me and my boss-- who hadn't had anything to do with the original content, or with the refresh so far. We talked about the use of AI narration. I laid out my argument: human narrators are easier to get a 'good take' out of. The first round of using TTS required countless iterations of tweaking phonemes in an XML subset called SSML, which I had written custom scripts to generate from the presentation text. Whereas correcting a human actor is as simple as saying "it's pronounced 'in-vest-i-ga-tor-ee'." My boss ignored me and said A.I. narration would be faster than recording a person, and the TTS results were only getting better. 

The project lead said they'd leave it up to us. Suddenly my boss was involved, and my coworkers were sending him the training material and CC'ing me second. My boss then churned out a narration on the free tier of Google's TTS from his personal account. Honestly, it's palatable; the voice flows naturally, and I didn't mind listening to it for the five minutes of the module.

I said as much to my boss. I also noted that, the way things are going for A.I. financially, we can't rely on stuff like Google's TTS staying free forever, or even available. My boss agreed and said, quote, "Google is in its drug-dealer phase." I agree. They and other A.I. booster companies are sinking untold billions in the hopes the general consumer will get hooked to spicy autocomplete, so we will pay for the privilege of wallowing in their slop like the little piggies we are. 

This gave me hope he would lean off using A.I. in the project. A few days later, I learned second-hand that he'd made a whole new presentation with Google's A.I. video editing suite with generated art and transitions, then told the project lead this was the standard we should be aspiring to for the entire content refresh. I wasn't invited to the meeting.

In subsequent back-and-forths with the project lead, my boss sent an A.I.-generated clip to be used as stock footage in the presentation. It featured a professional-looking white woman interviewing a white man who clearly was supposed to have Down Syndrome. But not _too much_ Down Syndrome. He looked Down Syndrome enough to keep the viewer comfortable. He's an imaginary, inoffensive avatar for the idea of Down Syndrome; the statistical mean of what millions of images tokenized by the underpaid labor third-worlders to the specifications of what some kale-smoothie drinking motherfucker in San Francisco thinks is a presentable version of Down Syndrome. 

One coworker's only feedback was, "can you remove the smiles from their faces? They're supposed to be talking about a serious situation."

Another coworker said they didn't like how the videos were clearly A.I.-generated. My boss replied, quote, "I've made peace with it." He sent another iteration of the video without the subjects smiling, and the first coworker approved.

If the enthusiasm for A.I. was unanimous from the unit, maybe I wouldn't be so frustrated. But that I have at least one coworker objecting to the content, and that my boss essentially steam-rolled over their objection with a "sorry you feel that way"-ass excuse makes me so fucking frustrated. 

I've put forward my material objections to A.I.: that the time spent generating a good output outweighs the time saved of working with real people; that the availability of the tools is unreliable, and we'll soon see them rocket up in price or disappear completely; and that the legislative landscape is changing, and it's possible genAI content will require copious disclaimers and restrictions (if, insallah, a ban isn't forthcoming).

## ideal schmideal

But my greatest objection is less strictly pragmatic, and therefore difficult to articulate in a professional setting. It's that the text and images and voices coming from these A.I. models are statistical averages meant to represent the _idea_ of something rather than something actually real, something with substance and consequence in the world, something which can be experienced and interpreted. GenAI reverses the formula: interpretation precedes existence.

I played with Microsoft Copilot's image generating site when my work account was granted access (Cancel me). I threw strange non-sequiters at it to try to get it to spit something strange out. More often than not, it was mediocre, air-brushed, middle-of-the road schlock-- and that was when it would generate anything, since just as often it would spin for a while then wag its finger at me: "this image has been blocked by our offensive content filters". And I wasn't even trying to squeeze porn or gore out of it. I barely managed to get it to show a man with a bloody stump for an arm by burying the description in a paragraph of nonsense.

What I was investigating, if naively, was if the model could produce novelty. Whatever that means. Maybe with great effort of the prompter's part can the AI vomit out something that makes you go, 'huh'. The points of failure are mildly interesting, but that's exactly what they are in the eyes of the models' corporate progenitors: failures. They want the models to produce images of things that conform to the *ideal* form of that thing; not things-in-themselves.

And if OpenAI and Google and my boss get their way, we'll soon be inculcated in a world of ideal forms-- but *whose* ideal? Not Black social workers in the South. Not abuse victims with Down Syndrome. Not the Kenyan tech workers on whose labor the models are trained[^1]. 

Everything not to the standards of white, United-Statesian marketers will cease to be admitted the right to public existence. It will not be, as the Twitter ghouls proclaim, a world in which each person is given her own personal media ecosystem, exactly to her taste, but one in which one's tastes are made to conform exactly to pale simulacra; a world in which every wart and wrinkle and frustration and horror of reality is cookie-cut into a soothing, palatable, professional-looking package, its smooth, air-brushed surface providing no purchase for spears of objection, spears which glance off acknowledge only by the phrase: "make peace with it."
